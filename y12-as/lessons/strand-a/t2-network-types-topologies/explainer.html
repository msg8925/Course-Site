01

LAN vs WAN

Networks are classified by how far they physically reach:

LAN (Local Area Network)WAN (Wide Area Network)
Geographic scopeA single site — one building or campusSpans multiple sites, cities, or countries
Typically owned byThe organisation itselfMade up of infrastructure leased from telecoms providers, connecting separate LANs together
ExampleAll the computers in one school buildingThat school's network connected to a sister school in another country, via the internet
A WAN is often just several LANs, joined up The internet itself is the largest WAN of all — a network of interconnected networks, each of which might itself be a LAN.
02

What is a topology?

A network topology is the pattern in which devices on a network are physically or logically connected to one another. The topology chosen affects three practical things: how the network behaves when a single cable or device fails, how much cabling it needs, and how easily it can be extended.

03

Star topology

Every device connects with its own individual cable to one central device — usually a switch. All data passes through that central point on its way to anywhere else on the network.

  • Advantage: if one cable fails, only the single device on that cable is affected — the rest of the network keeps working normally.
  • Disadvantage: if the central switch itself fails, the entire network goes down at once, since every device's only route runs through it.
  • Disadvantage: needs more cable overall than a bus topology, since every device needs its own run back to the centre.
04

Bus topology

Every device connects to a single shared cable — the backbone — that runs the length of the network. Data travels along this shared backbone to reach its destination.

  • Advantage: uses comparatively little cable, since there's only one backbone rather than a separate cable to a central point for every device.
  • Disadvantage: if the backbone cable itself is cut, the network is split into two disconnected segments — devices on the wrong side of the break can no longer reach each other.
  • Disadvantage: every device shares the same backbone, so heavy traffic from one device can slow things down for everyone else on it.
05

Mesh topology

In a full mesh, every device is connected directly to every other device. (A partial mesh connects only some pairs directly, relying on other devices to forward data the rest of the way — a middle ground between full mesh and the other topologies.)

  • Advantage: extremely resilient — if one cable fails, data can simply be rerouted via a different device, and the network keeps working.
  • Disadvantage: needs far more cable than any other topology, and that requirement grows very quickly as devices are added (section 08 puts a number on this).
06

Hybrid topology

A hybrid topology combines two or more of the basic topologies together — for example, several separate star networks (each with its own switch), with those switches themselves connected to each other along a shared backbone. This lets a design take advantage of the strengths of each part: star's fault isolation within each local cluster, combined with a simpler backbone connecting the clusters, rather than needing every single device wired directly back to one central point.

Counting the cables

Star needs exactly one cable per device. Full mesh needs one cable for every pair of devices — which grows far faster as the network gets bigger.
