01

What is a LAN?

A LAN (Local Area Network) is a network confined to a small geographic area — a classroom, a building, a school site — typically owned and controlled by one organisation, and offering high-speed connections between its devices.

Compare to Session 2Two directly-cabled PCs technically formed a tiny LAN already. Today we just make it bigger, and add the hardware that makes "bigger" practical.
02

What does a switch do?

A switch connects multiple devices on a LAN. Instead of every device needing a direct cable to every other device, each device connects to the switch — and the switch works out where each piece of data needs to go.

The switch does this by building its own MAC address table — a record of which device is connected to which physical port. This is completely separate from any individual PC's ARP table.

 A PC's ARP tableA switch's MAC table
MapsIP address → MAC addressMAC address → physical port
PurposeSo the PC knows who to send toSo the switch knows where to forward frames
03

ARP tables stay separate

Adding more PCs to the switch doesn't mean every PC suddenly knows about every other PC. Each PC only builds ARP entries for devices it has actually contacted.

04

Practical lab steps

  1. Build a switch-based LAN with 2 PCs; check each PC's arp -a (still empty).
  2. Ping between them; recheck ARP tables — entries appear as in Session 2.
  3. Add a third and fourth PC to the switch; ping in different pairs and predict/check how each PC's ARP table grows.
  4. Use Filius's data-exchange view to inspect a ping's ARP request/reply and ICMP echo request/reply as separate stages.
  5. Discuss what the switch itself is doing at each step.
