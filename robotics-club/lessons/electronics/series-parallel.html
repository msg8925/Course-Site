Series and Parallel Resistor Circuits

Section focus: Electronics Fundamentals — Lesson 2.3  |  Duration: ~45 minutes

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, you will be able to:

  • Explain the difference between a series and a parallel circuit
  • Calculate total resistance for resistors in series
  • Calculate total resistance for resistors in parallel
  • Predict which arrangement gives a higher or lower total resistance
Real-world analogy: A series circuit is like a single-lane road with two toll booths, one after another — every car pays both tolls, one at a time, with no way around either. A parallel circuit is like two separate lanes, each with its own toll booth — cars can choose either lane, so overall, more cars get through for the same amount of time, which is exactly the effect on current.

1. Series Circuits

In a series circuit, components are connected end-to-end, forming a single path — all the current must flow through every component in turn.

Figure 1: Two resistors in series — one continuous path for current.

Total resistance in series is simply the sum of every resistor:

R_total = R1 + R2 + R3 + ...

Example: a 220Ω and a 330Ω resistor in series: 220 + 330 = 550Ω total. Adding more resistors in series always increases total resistance — there's more restriction for current to fight through.

2. Parallel Circuits

In a parallel circuit, components are connected across the same two points, giving current more than one path to choose from.

Figure 2: Two resistors in parallel — current can flow through either path.

Total resistance in parallel uses a different formula, since adding a second path makes it easier overall for current to flow, not harder:

1 / R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...

Example: two 220Ω resistors in parallel: 1/R_total = 1/220 + 1/220 = 2/220, so R_total = 220/2 = 110Ω total.

The counterintuitive part: adding a second resistor in parallel makes the total resistance lower, not higher — the opposite of series. This surprises almost everyone the first time, since "adding more resistors" sounds like it should always add more resistance. Adding a second lane makes a road easier to get through overall, even though each individual lane still has its own toll booth.

3. A Handy Shortcut for Two Equal Resistors in Parallel

When exactly two resistors of equal value are in parallel, the total is always simply half of one resistor's value — as seen in the 220Ω example above (220 ÷ 2 = 110Ω). This shortcut only works for two equal resistors — different values need the full formula.

4. Why This Matters

Understanding series versus parallel isn't just theoretical — it explains circuits you've already built. The seven segments of a 7-segment display, for example, are each wired with their own series resistor rather than sharing one, precisely because different segments may be lit in different combinations at different times, and a shared resistor would cause each segment's brightness to depend on how many others were lit alongside it.

Try it yourself:
  1. Calculate the total resistance of a 100Ω and a 220Ω resistor in series.
  2. Calculate the total resistance of the same two resistors (100Ω and 220Ω) in parallel.
  3. If available, physically build both circuits on a breadboard and verify your calculations with a multimeter.
  4. Challenge: three resistors — 100Ω, 220Ω, and 330Ω — are all in parallel. Extend the parallel formula to three terms and calculate the total.
