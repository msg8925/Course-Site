Driving a DC Motor with a Single Transistor

Session focus: Motors — building your own simple motor driver  |  Duration: ~60 minutes

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, you will be able to:

  • Explain how a transistor can act as a switch controlled by a tiny signal
  • Wire a transistor to switch a DC motor on, off, and at variable speed
  • Explain why a flyback diode is essential, not optional
  • Identify what this circuit can't do compared to a dedicated driver chip like the L298N
Real-world analogy: A transistor here works like a foot-operated valve on a garden hose — a very light press with your foot (the small signal from the Arduino) controls a much larger flow of water (the motor's current) without your foot ever touching the water directly. This is exactly what the L298N does internally too — it's essentially a box containing several transistor-like switches. Building this circuit yourself shows you what's inside that "black box."
Important limitation before you start: this circuit can turn the motor on, off, and control its speed — but it cannot reverse direction. Reversing direction requires an H-bridge arrangement (four switches, not one), which is what the L298N provides. If your project needs the motor to run both forward and backward, use the L298N lesson instead — this circuit is for when one direction is enough, or for understanding the underlying principle.

2. Understanding the Transistor's Three Legs

A basic NPN transistor has three legs, each with a distinct job:

LegFunctionConnects To
BaseThe small "control" signal inputArduino PWM pin, via the 1kΩ resistor
CollectorWhere the larger current flows in fromMotor's negative terminal
EmitterWhere the larger current flows out toGND (shared between Arduino and battery pack)

A small current flowing into the base allows a much larger current to flow from collector to emitter — this amplification is exactly what lets a 40mA-capable Arduino pin control a motor that might draw several hundred milliamps.

Checking your transistor's pinout: Base/Collector/Emitter order varies between transistor models and packages — it is not universal. Look up the datasheet for your exact component (search its printed part number, e.g. "2N2222 pinout" or "TIP120 pinout") before wiring, rather than assuming.

3. Why the Flyback Diode Is Essential

Never skip this component. A motor's internal coil resists sudden changes in current. The instant you switch the transistor off, the coil fights back by producing a brief, sharp voltage spike — often far higher than your supply voltage — as it releases stored energy. Without a diode to safely redirect that spike, it can flow back into the transistor (and potentially the Arduino) and damage it, sometimes immediately, sometimes through gradual wear that causes mysterious failures weeks later. The diode, wired backwards across the motor's terminals, gives that spike a safe path to dissipate instead.

Figure 1: Arduino PWM pin drives the base; the transistor switches motor current from the battery; the diode protects against voltage spikes.

4. Step-by-Step Instructions

1Wire the transistor's control side

  • Arduino Pin 9 → 1kΩ resistor → transistor's base

2Wire the motor and power side

  • Battery pack positive → motor's positive terminal
  • Motor's negative terminal → transistor's collector
  • Transistor's emitter → GND
  • Diode connected across the motor's two terminals, oriented so it points from the transistor-side terminal toward the battery-side terminal (opposite to normal current flow) — check the diode's band matches this orientation

3Connect a common ground

The battery pack's negative terminal, the transistor's emitter, and the Arduino's GND pin must all be connected together. Without this shared reference point, the Arduino's control signal has no consistent "zero" to measure against, and the circuit won't behave predictably.

4Write the program

// Single-Transistor DC Motor Control
const int motorPin = 9;

void setup() {
  pinMode(motorPin, OUTPUT);
}

void loop() {
  analogWrite(motorPin, 255);  // full speed
  delay(2000);

  analogWrite(motorPin, 130);  // half speed
  delay(2000);

  analogWrite(motorPin, 0);    // off
  delay(2000);
}

5Power on and test

Upload the code, then connect the battery pack. The motor should run at full speed, then slow down, then stop, repeating in a loop. Notice this uses the same analogWrite() approach as the L298N's ENA pin — the underlying principle of PWM speed control hasn't changed, only what's doing the switching.

Troubleshooting

  • Motor doesn't run at all: double-check the transistor's base/collector/emitter pinout against its actual datasheet — an easy mix-up between models.
  • Motor runs but Arduino resets or behaves strangely: check the common ground connection between battery, transistor, and Arduino.
  • Transistor gets noticeably hot: confirm it's rated for more current than the motor draws when starting (stall current is much higher than running current).
  • Motor runs at full speed regardless of PWM value: check Pin 9 is genuinely PWM-capable and that you're using analogWrite(), not digitalWrite().

6. Why This Matters

Every motor driver chip you'll ever use — the L298N included — is fundamentally built from circuits like this one, just packaged neatly and arranged so groups of transistors can reverse current direction (an H-bridge). Understanding this simple version means the "black box" driver chip is no longer a mystery — it's just several of these switching circuits working together.

Try it yourself:
  1. Replace the fixed delay sequence with a potentiometer (from the earlier analog input lesson) controlling motor speed in real time.
  2. Research what an H-bridge is and sketch (on paper) how four transistors could be arranged to reverse a motor's direction.
  3. Challenge: compare the maximum speed and smoothness of this circuit against the L298N from the earlier lesson, using the same motor and battery pack. Are they noticeably different?
