Stepper Motors — How They Work

Section focus: Electronics Fundamentals — Lesson 2.9  |  Duration: ~40 minutes

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, you will be able to:

  • Explain what makes a stepper motor fundamentally different from the DC motor covered in Lesson 2.7
  • Explain how energising coils in sequence produces controlled, discrete movement
  • Explain why a stepper motor "knows" its position without any sensor
  • Connect this concept to the driver hardware you'll use to control one
Real-world analogy: Picture a ring of light bulbs arranged in a circle, with a magnet positioned nearby that's always attracted toward whichever bulb is currently lit. Light bulb 1, and the magnet moves to point at it. Turn off bulb 1 and light bulb 2 instead, and the magnet moves to point at that one — one small step at a time, in whichever order you switch the bulbs. Lighting them in sequence around the ring makes the magnet rotate, one precise step per switch.

1. The Core Difference from a DC Motor

Lesson 2.7 covered a DC motor that spins continuously and smoothly as long as power is applied — it has no built-in concept of "position," only "spinning" or "not spinning." A stepper motor is built entirely differently: instead of one continuously-spinning coil, it has several separate coils arranged around a central magnet, and it moves in small, fixed-size steps by energising those coils in a specific sequence, one (or a pair) at a time.

2. Stepping Through the Sequence

Figure 1: A simplified 4-coil stepper. Energising Coil B next (instead of A) would rotate the magnet 90° to point at B.

Real steppers (like the 28BYJ-48 in your kit) have far more than 4 coil positions internally, combined with internal gearing, which is why they take many small steps (often 2048 per full revolution) rather than four large ones — but the underlying principle shown above is exactly the same, just repeated many times around a finer circle.

3. Why a Stepper "Knows" Its Position

Because each step is a fixed, known size, and the motor only moves when a coil is explicitly switched, keeping a simple count of how many steps you've commanded tells you exactly where the motor's shaft is — without needing any separate position sensor. This is the single biggest reason to choose a stepper over a DC motor: precise, trackable position, at the cost of top speed and smooth continuous rotation.

Trade-off worth noting: that precision isn't free. A stepper motor is generally slower and less efficient than an equivalent-sized DC motor doing continuous rotation, and it draws current continuously just to hold a fixed position — a DC motor draws nothing at all once stopped. Choosing between them is a genuine engineering trade-off, not a simple "better/worse" choice.

4. Why Steppers Need a Dedicated Driver

Switching multiple coils in the correct rapid sequence is more complex than the single on/off switching a transistor provides for a DC motor. This is why stepper motors are typically paired with a dedicated driver chip (like the ULN2003 in your kit) — it handles switching the right coils in the right order at the right time, so your code only needs to say "step forward" or "step backward," not manage each individual coil.

Looking ahead: Lesson 3.13 (Stepper Motors, in the Arduino section) puts this into practice with your actual 28BYJ-48 motor and ULN2003 driver board, including a specific pin-ordering detail in the code that only makes sense once you understand the coil-sequencing idea from this lesson.
Try it yourself:
  1. Using the 4-coil diagram above, sketch what sequence of coils (A, B, C, D) would need to energise, in order, to rotate the magnet a full 360°.
  2. Research your kit's 28BYJ-48 motor's steps-per-revolution figure and calculate how many degrees a single step actually moves the shaft.
  3. Explain in your own words why a stepper motor uses more power to simply hold a position than a DC motor uses to hold nothing (i.e. sit stopped).
  4. Challenge: research the difference between "full-step" and "half-step" driving modes for a stepper motor, and explain what half-stepping achieves.
