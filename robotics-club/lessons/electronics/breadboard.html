How a Breadboard Works

Section focus: Electronics Fundamentals — Lesson 2.1  |  Duration: ~45 minutes

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, you will be able to:

  • Explain what a breadboard is for and why it's used instead of soldering for prototyping
  • Identify which holes on a breadboard are electrically connected to each other
  • Correctly place a component so it forms a working connection
  • Use the power rails to distribute 5V and GND across a circuit
Real-world analogy: A breadboard is like a box of building blocks for electricity — it lets you snap components together and take them apart again instantly, with no glue (solder) required. This makes it perfect for testing an idea, fixing mistakes, and trying variations before committing to a permanent version.

1. What's Actually Inside

Underneath the plastic, a breadboard is just a grid of tiny metal spring clips, arranged in short strips. When you push a wire or component leg into a hole, it grips that internal metal strip — and anything else pushed into the same strip is now electrically connected to it, as if joined by a wire.

Figure 1: Power rails run horizontally along the top and bottom; the main grid connects in short vertical columns, split by a centre gap.

2. The Two Types of Connection

AreaHow holes are connectedTypical use
Main grid (middle section)In short vertical columns of 5 holes eachPlacing components and jumper wires
Power rails (top and bottom strips)In long horizontal rows, usually marked with a red (+) and blue (−) lineDistributing power (5V and GND) to multiple parts of a circuit
The centre gap matters. Most breadboards have a gap running down the middle, splitting each row into a left half and a right half that are not connected to each other. This gap exists specifically so you can straddle a chip across it without shorting its two rows of pins together — a very common source of confusion for beginners who assume the whole row is one connection.

3. A Worked Example

Consider lighting an LED with a resistor, both plugged into the main grid:

  • The LED's leg and the resistor's leg, if placed in the same column, are now connected to each other — exactly as if you'd joined them with a wire.
  • A jumper wire from the power rail into that same column brings power into the circuit.
  • A jumper wire from another part of the circuit into the blue (−) rail sends current back to ground.

This is exactly the pattern you'll use in nearly every lesson from here on — a component's leg, a resistor's leg, and a wire, all sharing one column to form a single electrical node.

Checking your work: if a circuit doesn't work, the very first thing to check is which column or rail each leg actually landed in — a leg one row or column off from where you intended is the single most common wiring mistake, and it often looks correct at a glance.
Try it yourself:
  1. Take a single jumper wire and push both ends into the same vertical column, a few holes apart. Use a multimeter (if available) in continuity mode to confirm both ends are connected.
  2. Try the same test across the centre gap — confirm the two halves are not connected.
  3. Trace the power rails along their full length and confirm they stay connected across the entire board (rather than in short columns like the main grid).
  4. Challenge: without any code or power, physically place an LED and a resistor in the same column on the breadboard, ready to be wired to a power source — you'll power it up properly in Lesson 2.5.
