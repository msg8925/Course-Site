Capacitors and RC Circuits

Section focus: Electronics Fundamentals — Lesson 2.5  |  Duration: ~45 minutes

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, you will be able to:

  • Explain what a capacitor stores and how that differs from a battery
  • Identify a capacitor's two states: charging and discharging
  • Explain what an RC circuit is and why the resistor's value affects its timing
  • Predict, roughly, how changing resistance or capacitance affects charge time
Real-world analogy: A capacitor is like a small water tank connected to a pipe. A narrow pipe (a large resistor) fills or drains the tank slowly; a wide pipe (a small resistor) fills or drains it quickly. The tank (capacitor) stores a limited amount of water (charge) and releases it back out once the input stops — unlike a water tower's constant supply (a battery), a tank empties as soon as you stop filling it and start drawing from it.

1. What a Capacitor Actually Stores

A capacitor stores electrical charge directly, using two conductive plates separated by a thin insulating layer. This is meaningfully different from a battery, which stores energy chemically and releases it steadily over a long time. A capacitor charges and discharges much faster, but usually holds far less energy overall — it's built for short bursts and precise timing, not long-term power storage.

2. Charging and Discharging

Connect a capacitor to a power source through a resistor, and it doesn't charge instantly — it fills gradually, fastest at first and slowing down as it approaches full charge:

Figure 1: Charging curve — fast at first, then gradually levelling off as the capacitor approaches full charge.

Discharging follows the mirror image of this curve — fast at first, then slowing as it approaches empty.

3. The RC Circuit and Time Constant

An RC circuit is simply a resistor (R) and a capacitor (C) wired together — the combination that produces exactly the charging behaviour shown above. How fast this happens is described by a value called the time constant, calculated as:

τ (tau) = R × C

Where R is resistance in ohms and C is capacitance in farads, giving τ in seconds. One time constant is roughly how long it takes the capacitor to reach about 63% of full charge — not fully charged, but a useful, consistent reference point for comparing circuits.

ChangeEffect on charging speed
Larger resistorSlower — less current can flow in to fill the capacitor
Smaller resistorFaster — more current can flow in
Larger capacitorSlower — there's simply more capacity to fill
Smaller capacitorFaster — less capacity to fill
A concrete example: a 10kΩ resistor and a 100µF (microfarad) capacitor give τ = 10,000 × 0.0001 = 1 second. Swap in a 1kΩ resistor instead, and τ drops to 0.1 seconds — ten times faster, matching the ten-times-smaller resistance.

4. Why Capacitors Matter in Practice

You've actually already met one reason capacitors matter, even without a dedicated lesson: the flyback diode from the transistor motor lesson protects against a sudden voltage spike; a capacitor placed near a motor or relay can additionally help smooth out rapid, noisy fluctuations in voltage, for a similar protective reason. RC circuits like this one also show up in simple timing circuits, basic filters that separate fast-changing signals from slow ones, and button "debouncing" — cleaning up the rapid, unwanted electrical noise created by a mechanical switch closing.

Try it yourself:
  1. Calculate the time constant for a 1kΩ resistor and a 1000µF capacitor.
  2. If your calculated time constant doubled, would the capacitor charge faster or slower? Explain why in your own words.
  3. Look up (or ask) what "microfarad" (µF) means in relation to a farad, and convert 100µF into farads.
  4. Challenge: research what "debouncing" means for a mechanical switch, and explain in a sentence or two how an RC circuit could help solve that problem.
