How a Transistor Works

Section focus: Electronics Fundamentals — Lesson 2.6  |  Duration: ~45 minutes

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, you will be able to:

  • Explain what a transistor does, at a basic level, without needing semiconductor physics
  • Identify a transistor's three legs and their roles
  • Build a simple circuit where a small signal switches a larger current on and off
  • Explain why this matters for controlling motors, relays, and other high-current devices from an Arduino
Real-world analogy: A transistor is like a foot-operated valve on a large water pipe. A light press of your foot (a small electrical signal) controls a much larger flow of water (a much larger current) — your foot never touches the water directly, and the amount of effort needed to press the valve has nothing to do with how much water is actually flowing through the pipe.

1. The Problem a Transistor Solves

An Arduino pin can only safely supply a very small amount of current — roughly 20-40mA. Many useful things you'd want to control — motors, bright lights, relays — need far more current than that. Connecting them directly to a pin would damage the Arduino almost immediately. A transistor solves this by acting as a current-controlled switch: a tiny, Arduino-safe current controls a much larger current flowing through a completely separate part of the circuit.

2. The Three Legs of an NPN Transistor

The most common basic transistor type, NPN, has three legs, each with a distinct job:

Figure 1: A small current into the Base allows a much larger current to flow from Collector to Emitter.

LegRoleTypically connects to
BaseThe small control signal inputAn Arduino pin, via a resistor
CollectorWhere the larger current entersThe load being controlled (e.g. a motor)
EmitterWhere the larger current exitsGND
Pinout is not universal. Which physical leg is Base, Collector, or Emitter varies between transistor models and packages — always check the datasheet for your specific component (search its printed part number) rather than assuming a standard order.

3. Switch, Not Amplifier — For Now

Transistors can be used in more advanced ways to amplify a signal proportionally (used in audio equipment, for example), but for this club's purposes, you'll mostly use them in a simpler mode: fully on or fully off, controlled by whether the base is receiving a signal. Give the base close to no current, and almost no current flows through the collector-emitter path either — the transistor is "off." Give the base enough current, and a much larger current is allowed to flow through — the transistor is "on."

4. Why a Base Resistor Is Necessary

Just like an LED, a transistor's base can be damaged by too much current flowing into it directly. A resistor (commonly around 1kΩ) placed between the Arduino pin and the base limits this to a safe level — exactly the same principle as the LED's series resistor from Lesson 2.4, just protecting a different component.

5. A Simple Switching Circuit

Picture a transistor switching an LED (a scaled-down stand-in for a motor, safe to test without extra hardware):

  • Arduino pin → 1kΩ resistor → Base
  • 5V → LED → 220Ω resistor → Collector
  • Emitter → GND

Setting the Arduino pin HIGH allows the base current to flow, switching the transistor on, which allows current to flow through the LED. Setting the pin LOW switches it back off. Nothing here is fundamentally different from an LED connected directly to a pin — the point of this exercise is purely to see the transistor behaving as a switch on a load you can already predict, before trusting it with something bigger.

Looking ahead: the next lesson, How a DC Motor Works, and the lesson after that, Driving a DC Motor with a Transistor, both build directly on what you've just learned here — a motor is simply a bigger, less forgiving load than an LED, and it introduces one additional safety component (a flyback diode) that an LED circuit doesn't need.
Try it yourself:
  1. Look up your kit's transistor's exact part number and find its datasheet's pinout diagram.
  2. Build the LED-switching circuit described above and confirm it turns on and off as expected.
  3. Try removing the base resistor briefly (with caution) — research why this is risky rather than trying it if you're not confident.
  4. Challenge: instead of a fixed HIGH/LOW, use analogWrite() on the base pin, and observe whether the LED's brightness changes — does the transistor behave as a simple switch, or does it show some in-between behaviour?
