Building a Simple LED Circuit

Section focus: Electronics Fundamentals — Lesson 2.4  |  Duration: ~45 minutes

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, you will be able to:

  • Identify an LED's two legs and explain why orientation matters
  • Explain why an LED needs a series resistor and roughly how to size one
  • Build a complete, working LED circuit on a breadboard from a battery, with no Arduino or code involved
  • Diagnose the most common reasons an LED circuit doesn't light up
Real-world analogy: An LED is like a one-way turnstile for electricity — current can only flow through it in one direction. Push it the "wrong" way, and nothing happens at all, no matter how much power is available; this is completely normal and not a fault.

1. Identifying the LED's Legs

LegNameHow to identify it
Longer legAnode (+)Connects toward the positive supply
Shorter legCathode (−)Connects toward ground; also usually has a small flat edge on that side of the LED's plastic dome
If both legs have been trimmed to the same length (common in kits or after reuse), look instead for the flat edge on the dome, or check for a slightly larger internal metal piece visible through the plastic on the cathode side. If truly unsure, a multimeter's diode-test mode will light the LED briefly to reveal its orientation.

2. Why the Series Resistor Is Necessary

An LED has almost no natural resistance of its own — connected directly across a power source with nothing else in the circuit, it will draw far more current than it's rated for, and fail almost immediately, sometimes with a visible flash or crack. A resistor placed in series limits current to a safe level, exactly as covered in Lesson 2.2.

Roughly sizing a resistor: a common, simple approach for a standard LED on a 5V supply is a 220Ω–330Ω resistor — this is why you've seen 220Ω used repeatedly across earlier lessons. A more precise calculation exists (based on the LED's exact forward voltage and desired current), but 220Ω is a safe, standard default for the components in this club.

3. Step-by-Step: Building the Circuit

1Identify your components

One LED, one 220Ω resistor, a breadboard, and a 5V power source (a battery pack works fine — no Arduino needed for this lesson).

2Place the LED

Push the LED into the breadboard's main grid so its two legs sit in two separate columns.

3Add the resistor

Connect the resistor in series with the LED's anode (longer leg) — one end in the same column as the anode, the other end running toward your positive power connection.

4Complete the circuit

Connect the LED's cathode (shorter leg) to your power source's ground/negative terminal.

5Connect power and test

Connect your power source. The LED should light immediately — there's no code or delay involved, since this is a purely electrical circuit.

Troubleshooting

  • Nothing lights up: the most common cause by far is the LED being backwards — try flipping it around.
  • Still nothing: check the resistor and LED are actually in the same column as intended (see the breadboard lesson) rather than one row or column off.
  • LED is extremely dim: check your power source is actually delivering close to 5V, and that connections are solid.
  • LED flashed briefly then died: this usually means the resistor was missing or wired incorrectly, allowing too much current through — unfortunately, this typically destroys the LED, so try a fresh one with the resistor properly in place.

4. Why This Matters

This exact circuit — LED, series resistor, power, ground — is the electrical foundation underneath every LED-based Arduino lesson you've done or will do, including the RGB LED and 7-segment display lessons. The only thing Arduino adds on top is replacing the fixed battery connection with a pin the code can turn on, off, or dim — the LED and resistor themselves work identically either way.

Try it yourself:
  1. Build the circuit exactly as described and confirm it lights up.
  2. Deliberately reverse the LED and confirm it stays dark — then flip it back.
  3. Swap the 220Ω resistor for a much larger one (e.g. 10kΩ) and observe the difference in brightness.
  4. Challenge: build two LED circuits side by side, sharing the same power source, and predict (using Lesson 2.3) whether you've wired them in series or parallel based on how they behave if you remove one LED.
