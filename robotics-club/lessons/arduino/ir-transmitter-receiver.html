Building an IR Reflectance Sensor

Session focus: Sensors — infrared LED + phototransistor for surface detection  |  Duration: ~60 minutes

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, you will be able to:

  • Explain how an IR LED and phototransistor work together to detect a surface
  • Wire the pair to an Arduino using a simple voltage-divider circuit
  • Read and interpret the analog signal to tell light surfaces from dark ones
  • Calibrate a threshold value for reliable detection
Real-world analogy: This works like shining a torch at the floor at night and watching how much light bounces back into your eyes. A white or light-coloured floor bounces most of the light back — it looks bright. A black surface absorbs most of the light instead of reflecting it — it looks dark. The IR LED is your torch (just invisible to human eyes), and the phototransistor is the "eye" watching how much bounces back.

Telling the LED and phototransistor apart: They often look identical to the eye. Two clues help: the phototransistor usually has a flat side or slightly different lens tint, and it's clearly marked in most starter kits. If in doubt, check with your teacher before wiring — connecting them the wrong way round won't damage anything, but the circuit won't work as expected.

2. Understanding the Two Halves of the Circuit

This sensor is really two small circuits working as a pair:

ComponentRoleCircuit type
IR LEDConstantly shines invisible infrared light downwardSimple LED circuit — resistor in series, straight to 5V and GND
IR PhototransistorActs like a light-dependent switch — the more IR light hits it, the more current it lets throughVoltage divider with a 10kΩ resistor, output read as an analog voltage
How the phototransistor's voltage divider works: The phototransistor and the 10kΩ resistor are wired in series between 5V and GND, with the analog pin connected at the junction between them. More reflected IR light makes the phototransistor conduct more, pulling that junction voltage toward one end; less light lets the resistor pull it toward the other end. This is the same voltage-divider idea as the potentiometer lesson — the difference is that here, light (not a knob) is what changes the "resistance."

3. Step-by-Step Instructions

1Wire the IR LED

  • IR LED's longer leg (anode) → 220Ω resistor → Arduino 5V
  • IR LED's shorter leg (cathode) → Arduino GND

This LED stays on continuously — there's no need to control it with a pin for this lesson.

2Wire the phototransistor

  • Phototransistor's collector leg → Arduino 5V
  • Phototransistor's emitter leg → 10kΩ resistor → Arduino GND
  • Junction between the phototransistor and the 10kΩ resistor → Arduino A0

3Position the sensor

Mount or hold the LED and phototransistor pointing downward, close together, a few millimetres to a couple of centimetres above the surface. Too far away and too little light reflects back to give a useful reading.

4Write the program

// IR Reflectance Sensor — Raw Reading
const int sensorPin = A0;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(9600);
}

void loop() {
  int reading = analogRead(sensorPin);
  Serial.println(reading);
  delay(100);
}

5Calibrate a threshold

Upload the code and open the Serial Monitor. Hold the sensor over a light/white surface and note the typical reading, then over a dark/black surface and note that reading too. Pick a threshold roughly halfway between the two:

// IR Reflectance Sensor — Light vs Dark Detection
const int sensorPin = A0;
const int threshold = 500;  // adjust based on YOUR calibration readings

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(9600);
}

void loop() {
  int reading = analogRead(sensorPin);

  if (reading > threshold) {
    Serial.println("Dark surface detected");
  } else {
    Serial.println("Light surface detected");
  }

  delay(100);
}
Whether "dark" reads high or low depends on your wiring: With the phototransistor's collector on 5V (as wired above), more reflected light means more current flows, pulling A0 toward 5V — so a light surface typically gives a higher reading and a dark surface a lower one. If your results come out the other way round, that's fine — it just means your specific wiring or component behaves the opposite way. Trust your own calibration readings over any assumption about which direction is "correct."

Troubleshooting

  • Reading barely changes between surfaces: the sensor is likely too far from the surface, or the IR LED and phototransistor aren't angled toward the same spot.
  • Reading is very noisy/jumpy: strong ambient light (especially sunlight, which contains a lot of infrared) can interfere — try shading the sensor or testing away from direct light.
  • No change at all, ever: double-check the phototransistor's collector/emitter aren't swapped, and that the LED and resistor values are correct.
  • Always reads close to 0 or close to 1023: check the 10kΩ resistor is actually in the circuit — without it, the voltage divider doesn't work.

4. Why This Matters

This is the exact sensing principle behind line-following: a strip of black tape on a light floor (or vice versa) reflects IR light differently to the surrounding surface, and this simple circuit is how the vehicle will "see" the line. Everything from here is refinement — multiple sensors side by side, faster reading, and using the detected pattern to steer via the motors from earlier sessions.

Try it yourself:
  1. Test a few different surfaces (paper, wood, black tape, coloured card) and record the readings for each.
  2. Adjust your threshold value based on the actual numbers you recorded, rather than the example value of 500.
  3. Challenge: combine this with the DC motor lesson — write a program that drives a motor forward while a light surface is detected, and stops the instant a dark surface (the line) is seen.
