Testing, Calibration, and Competition Rules

Section focus: Robotic Vehicle — Assembly & Competition Prep — Lesson 4.4.2  |  Duration: ~40 minutes

Builds on: the fully assembled vehicle from Lesson 4.4.1. This lesson is about systematic testing and understanding how the vehicle will actually be judged, rather than new hardware or code.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, you will be able to:

  • Run a structured test checklist rather than ad-hoc trial and error
  • Diagnose common whole-vehicle problems systematically, subsystem by subsystem
  • Explain how the end-of-year competition is judged
  • Identify what to prepare and check the day before competing

1. A Structured Testing Checklist

Rather than simply placing the finished vehicle on tape and hoping, work through subsystems in isolation first, exactly the order they were built:

CheckHow
Sensors read correctlyRe-run Lesson 4.1.2's raw readout code; confirm all sensors respond individually
Thresholds still accurateRe-run Lesson 4.1.4's calibration on the actual competition surface, at the vehicle's final mounted height
Motors respond correctlyRe-run Lesson 4.2.2's movement functions individually — forward, stop, each turn
Full logic on a straight lineTest the complete program on a straight tape section first
Full logic on curvesProgressively test gentler to sharper curves
Full run, start to finishRun the complete intended course multiple times, noting where and how failures happen
Why isolate subsystems again, even after Lesson 4.4.1's assembly testing? A problem that appears only once everything is combined is far easier to diagnose if you already know each subsystem worked correctly on its own — you can immediately rule out sensors or motors individually and focus on the interaction between them (usually the decision logic, or a wiring issue introduced during final assembly).

2. Systematic Troubleshooting

SymptomCheck first
Vehicle doesn't move at allBattery pack switched on and charged; motor wiring unchanged since testing
Vehicle moves but ignores the line entirelySensor readings and thresholds — recalibrate on the actual competition surface if not already done
Vehicle follows line at home, fails at a new locationLighting conditions affecting IR readings — recalibrate on-site rather than assuming home calibration transfers
Vehicle worked yesterday, fails today with no changes madeBattery charge level — motors and logic can behave differently as battery voltage drops

3. How the Competition Is Judged

The end-of-year competition day is open to students, parents, and faculty, with prizes for categories including best design and fastest completion time. Keep both in mind while finishing your build, not just raw speed:

  • Fastest completion time rewards a vehicle that reliably completes the course without losing the line — a fast vehicle that frequently derails will lose to a slower, more consistent one
  • Best design considers the build quality, wiring neatness, and overall presentation of the finished vehicle, not just its raw performance
Consistency beats raw speed. A vehicle tuned to be as fast as possible often becomes less stable on curves — before competition day, consider testing at a slightly reduced baseSpeed (Lesson 4.3.3) if it noticeably improves reliability. A guaranteed finish at a moderate speed typically scores better than a fast run with a high chance of losing the line.

4. Preparing for Competition Day

  • Charge or replace batteries the night before, not the morning of
  • Bring spare jumper wires, resistors, and tape in case of last-minute repairs
  • Recalibrate sensors on-site if lighting conditions differ from your usual testing space
  • Do a full practice run on-site before the actual judged attempt, if time allows
  • Keep your code backed up (e.g. emailed to yourself, or saved to more than one location) in case of a laptop issue on the day
Try it yourself:
  1. Work through the full structured testing checklist above, subsystem by subsystem, on your assembled vehicle.
  2. Time several full runs of your intended course and record both completion time and whether the line was lost at any point.
  3. Experiment with reducing baseSpeed slightly and compare consistency across multiple runs, not just a single best time.
  4. Challenge: write a short README or one-page summary of your vehicle's design choices — sensor count, steering approach, any unique features — ready to explain to judges on competition day.
